# Composition

Each landing of the camera is judged as a still photograph: the viewer's eye should go where you want, in the order you
want, and the frame should say the line's idea at a glance, even at phone size with the sound off.

## The frame spec

Write each landing as: the subject and its size; where it sits (which third, which side); the camera's height and
angle; the lens; foreground, middle and background; what's at the edges. Then measure the rendered frame against it
(`tools/blender/framing.py` gives sizes and positions).

## Placement

- **Thirds.** Eyes near the upper third line; the subject on a vertical third, not the centre. Centre framing is for
  deliberate symmetry (a corridor, a face-on confrontation, a graphic composition).
- **Lead room.** More space on the side the subject looks or moves toward (at least a third of the frame). Less space
  ahead makes them feel trapped, which is sometimes the point.
- **Headroom.** A little space above the head in medium shots; in close-ups the top crop goes through the forehead or
  hair on purpose, or clears the head.

## Depth

- **Layers.** A foreground (often soft and cropped by the edge), the subject (sharp), and a background that gives
  context. At least two, ideally three.
- **Depth cues:** overlap, size falloff, atmospheric haze, focus, light falling off with distance, converging lines.
- **Frames within the frame:** doorways, windows, shoulders, arches, the gap between two objects. They direct the eye and
  add depth.

## Lines and shapes

- **Leading lines** (roads, rails, shelves, rows of people, beams of light) guide the eye to the subject. Diagonals feel
  dynamic; horizontals calm; verticals stately.
- **Shapes** carry feeling: circles soft, triangles tense, squares stable. A composition built on a strong shape reads
  instantly.

## Balance and weight

Bright things, saturated things, faces, text and isolated things carry visual weight. Balance them across the frame, or
unbalance on purpose for tension. Negative space should be chosen: where the subject looks, where something will enter,
the emptiness that is the point.

## Separation

The subject reads against what's behind it, by value (light against dark), colour (warm against cool), focus, or edge
light. Check for merges: a dark head against a dark wall, a figure the same colour as the crowd around it. The subject is
usually the brightest or highest-contrast thing in the frame.

## Edges

- Nothing important within about 5% of an edge unless it's entering or leaving.
- No tangents: an object edge just touching the frame edge or another object's edge creates tension in the wrong place.
- Crop people between joints (mid-thigh, mid-forearm) rather than at them (knees, wrists, ankles), which reads as an
  amputation.
- A level horizon, unless the roll is deliberate (a banking flight, unease).

## Variety between frames

Neighbouring landings should differ in at least two of: size, camera height, side of the subject, lens, the subject's
place in frame. A sequence of landings should read as a progression (wide, closer, closest at the peak, then wide
again), not a column of the same frame.

## The one-glance test

Shrink the frame to 360 pixels wide. Does it still say what the moment is about? If it needs the words to make sense,
the frame isn't doing its job yet.

## Aspect ratios

16:9 for the main film. If a vertical 9:16 version is wanted, design it as its own composition (subject centred
horizontally, the key action in the middle 60%), not a crop of the wide frame.
