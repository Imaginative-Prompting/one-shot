# Editing and pacing

Even a film with one continuous camera is edited: its rhythm, its structure and its changes over time are decisions.
This is how the whole holds together.

## What a cut (or a shot change) has to do

Walter Murch's priorities for a cut, most important first: it serves the **emotion** of the moment; it advances the
**story**; it falls at a moment that's **rhythmically** right; it respects the viewer's **eye-trace** (where they were
looking in the last shot); it respects the screen's **two-dimensional** layout (directions, positions); it respects the
**three-dimensional** space of the action. When you can't have them all, give up the bottom ones first. A cut that
breaks continuity but lands the feeling is better than one that's tidy and dead.

## Rhythm

- **Shot length follows the music's density.** Busy, fast sections carry shorter shots or faster moves; slow sections
  let shots breathe. Vary length inside a section too; a metronome of equal shots is dull.
- **Build.** Sections often accelerate toward their end and the chorus, then release.
- **Rest.** After intensity, a moment of calm makes the next peak hit harder.

## Intensity over the whole film

Plot the film's intensity (camera speed, cut rate, contrast, saturation, how much moves, how close we are) against the
song's energy from `map.json`. The two curves should broadly agree, with the film's biggest moment on the song's biggest
moment, and with deliberate exceptions (a still, quiet image over the loudest bar can be devastating). A film at one
intensity throughout numbs the viewer.

## Contrast and affinity (Bruce Block)

Every visual component (space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement, rhythm) can be similar between shots (affinity:
calm, unified) or different (contrast: energetic, intense). Plan where the film uses each. Increase contrast toward peaks
and the turn; use affinity to make a section feel whole.

## Eye-trace and continuity

At a change of shot, the viewer's eye is still where the last shot left it. Putting the next shot's subject near the
same place makes the change smooth; putting it on the opposite side jolts. Keep screen direction consistent (someone
walking left to right keeps walking left to right) unless the change of direction is the point.

## Seams

Where shots meet, light, colour and motion should flow unless the jump is deliberate. A small shift in brightness or a
colour cast at a seam reads as an error. Check every seam by stepping across it.

## The opening and the ending

- **The first seconds** decide whether someone keeps watching. The opening shot is already the film at its best: a
  striking image, motion, a question.
- **The ending** resolves. It lands on the song's last note or holds on a final image through the tail. It never
  replays the opening unless the echo is the point, and then something about it has changed.

## The final watch

Watch the whole film at speed, without stopping, as a viewer would. Then step through every seam. Note every moment where
attention drifts, and fix the shot, not the note.
