# Lyric

A lyric is words to be sung over music and heard once, at speed. **How it sounds comes before everything else**: the
words have to flow, rhyme and sit on the beat, and the story has to be simple enough that anyone can follow what's
happening on first hearing. A lyric that's accurate and clever but clunky to sing has failed.

## Sound first

- **Write like a songwriter, not a reporter.** A song isn't a summary of events. It's a voice with a point of view,
  feelings, pictures and a hook. Pick the few moments that carry the story and let everything else go.
- **Short, even lines.** Most lines are 6 to 10 syllables. Lines in the same place in each verse have about the same
  count (within one or two), so the melody can repeat. A long line turns into talking.
- **A steady beat.** Say every line aloud over a steady pulse (da-DUM da-DUM). Natural word stress falls on the strong
  beats. If you have to bend a word's stress or cram syllables to make it fit, rewrite the line.
- **Rhymes you can hear.** Line ends that are meant to rhyme really rhyme: perfect rhymes (night / light) or close ones
  (home / alone). Weak pairs (profit / of it, said / instead stretched across a long line) sound like mistakes when sung.
  Decide each section's scheme (couplets AABB, or ABAB) and keep it. Internal rhymes and echoes add polish, but only
  after the end rhymes are strong.
- **Easy words.** Words anyone says out loud. No dates, no figures, no tickers, no full names of people most listeners
  don't know, no jargon. "The stock fell hard" sings; "Nvidia's lost value at one point seven trillion" doesn't.
- **Room to breathe.** Leave gaps. Held notes want open vowels (oh, ah, ay, ee) and no consonant pile-up at the end.
- **Repetition is a feature.** Hooks, refrains and repeated lines are how songs stick. A chorus is sung the same way
  each time (or changed on purpose at the end).

## Written in the song's genre

The lyric is written for a sound, chosen with the concept before any line, and the genre's way with words is part of
that sound. Some examples:

- **Country:** plain storytelling, concrete everyday detail, a twist on a common phrase as the hook.
- **Hip-hop and rap:** dense internal and multisyllabic rhyme, wordplay, rhythm that rides the beat.
- **Pop:** short, simple, repeatable lines; a hook said many times; direct emotion; open vowels for big notes.
- **Rock and pop-punk:** blunt, punchy, conversational; shouted hooks; short lines.
- **Folk:** narrative, imagery from place, a slower unfolding.
- **R&B and soul:** intimate, melisma-friendly vowels, conversational asides.
- **Musical theatre:** a character singing their want, wit and clear story beats.
- **Electronic and dance:** few words, chant-like repetition.

Write every section in that voice, verses as much as the chorus.

## The story, simply

- **Anyone can follow it.** Someone who knows nothing about the topic should understand what's happening in every
  section on first hearing. If a line needs background to make sense, it's the wrong line.
- **A few moments, not a list.** A song tells a story in three or four beats, one or two per section. A run of
  references back to back is a list, and lists don't move anyone.
- **Each section has a job.** Verse: moves the story with a specific picture. Pre-chorus (optional): lifts toward the
  chorus. Chorus: the idea in its simplest, most singable form, with the title or hook. Bridge: the turn, a new angle.
  Outro: the last image, changed by everything before it.
- **Start inside the story,** at full energy. The first line should make someone want the second.

## References and deep cuts: seasoning, not the meal

A few specific references to the topic make a song feel written by someone who knows the world: a famous phrase, a
moment everyone saw, a joke the community shares. Use a handful, where they sound good, phrased so a newcomer still
follows the line. They're never the reason a line exists, and the lyric is never a test of how much you know. Precise
details (numbers, dates, names, sources) go in the picture, not the song. A reference that doesn't sing well comes out,
however true it is. Everything sung is still true: keep it simple and true rather than precise and clunky.

## Metaphor

A good metaphor says more than a plain statement, sticks in the memory, and gives the film a picture. One clear image
developed through a section or the whole song is often the strongest choice for a music video, because the film can
build it. Keep each one clear enough to read on first hearing; don't mix two in one line.

## The hook

The hook is the phrase people remember: short, singable, and it says what the song is. It goes where the music is
strongest (the start or end of the chorus) and repeats. The title is usually the hook. Write the chorus first; the
verses lead to it.

## Writing for a music video

The song and the picture tell the story together. The lyric doesn't have to say everything: the picture can show a
contrast, a switch between sides, the specifics, the joke. Don't announce what the picture will make obvious ("good
news!", "now the other side"). The song still stands on its own: someone hearing it without the video follows it.

While writing each line, picture the scene it could become. Lines that suggest a picture give the film something to do.
Note a scene for each line as you go; stage 3 starts from them.

## Counting

At B beats a minute, one bar of four beats lasts 240 / B seconds; a verse line usually takes one or two bars. Sections
of 4, 8 or 16 bars are standard. Work out how many lines fit the target length, leaving room for a short intro, breaks
and the ending. Suno stretches a lyric that's short for the length asked and rushes one that's long.

## Checking it

`tools/lyric.py` reports what the ear would catch: line ends in each section that don't rhyme with anything near them,
rhymes that are only vowel matches, lines much longer than their neighbours, and lines dense with names and numbers. Fix
what it flags, then read the whole lyric aloud to a steady beat. If any line makes you stumble, rewrite it.
