# Scenes and coverage

A film is built from **scenes** and **shots**. A scene is one environment (a place, a world, a set) where something
happens; several shots live in it. A shot is one camera angle and framing within a scene, focused on something. The big
transitions of the film happen between scenes; inside a scene, shots move the viewer's attention around the place.

None of this is a rule. Each concept below exists because it helps a viewer follow and feel what's happening; use it when
it does, and break it when breaking it does more. Anything applied every time, whatever the moment, turns into slop.

## Scenes

- **A scene is a place worth being in.** It has its own look, light, palette and life, and it holds the viewer long
  enough to feel like somewhere: usually several lines, with several shots. A verse typically has a few scenes; a
  section can live in one.
- **Changing scene is a big event.** It's the film's strongest punctuation, so put scene changes where the song and the
  story change (a section, a turn, a new idea) and make the transition between scenes the most considered ones
  (`craft/transitions.md`).
- **Coming back** to an earlier scene is a strong device: the same place changed by what's happened since (the light, the
  season, who's there, what's broken). It's never required.
- **One scene per line** is a trap: nothing is established, nothing lives long enough to matter, and the film becomes a
  slideshow of sets.

## Coverage inside a scene

The shots a scene is made of, and the order the viewer meets them in:

- **Establishing shot.** A wide view that shows where we are, and how big it is, before going closer. Useful the first
  time we meet a place. Skip it when the place is already known, when withholding it builds mystery, or when the
  scene opens better on a detail and reveals the place later.
- **Master and closer coverage.** A wide that holds the whole action, then mediums and close-ups that pick out what
  matters. Going closer means more intensity; going wider releases it or reveals.
- **Inserts and cutaways.** A close-up of a detail (a hand, a screen, an object that matters) or a glance at something
  else in the scene. They carry the specifics: the deep cuts, the readable text, the punchline.
- **Shot / reverse shot.** Two people or things facing each other, each seen from over the other's shoulder. It makes
  a relationship or a confrontation.
- **Point of view.** What a character sees, framed as they see it, often after a shot of them looking.
- **Reveal order.** Decide what the viewer knows at each shot: start wide and push to the one thing, or start on a detail
  and pull out to show what it belongs to.

## Continuity

So the viewer always knows where they are and where things are:

- **The line of action (180°).** Keep the camera on one side of the line between two subjects (or along a direction of
  travel), so left stays left and right stays right. Cross it on purpose, with a move the viewer sees, or to disorient.
- **Screen direction.** Something moving left to right keeps moving left to right across shots, unless it turns around.
- **A real change of angle.** Neighbouring shots of the same subject differ by more than about 30° or a clear change of
  size; otherwise the change reads as a jump.
- **Matching action.** When an action continues across a change of shot, it continues from where it was.
- **Eye-trace.** The viewer's eye is still where the last shot left it; put the next point of interest near there for
  a smooth change, far from it for a jolt.

## With one continuous camera

The same ideas apply when the camera never cuts: within a scene it still establishes, goes closer, picks out details and
reveals; the "shots" are the compositions it lands on. Between scenes it makes the big transitions as moves
(`craft/camera.md`, "One continuous camera").
