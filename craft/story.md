# Story

A music video tells a story twice at once: in the song, through words and music, and in the film, through pictures. They
share one idea and support each other; neither just repeats the other.

## The one idea

Everything starts from a single idea you can say in a sentence: what this is about, and what someone should feel by the
end. A film with three ideas is three short films stitched together. *Check:* write the sentence. If it needs "and"
twice, choose.

## Shape

- **A turn.** Most stories that hold attention change direction once: a realisation, a loss, a reversal, a reveal. In a
  song it usually falls between the second chorus and the last (the bridge), and the music changes with it. Know where
  yours is before writing a line. *Break it when* the story is a steady build (a list, a journey, a crescendo); then the
  shape is accumulation, and each section has to be bigger than the last.
- **Start inside the story.** The first line and the first frame are already the story at full strength. Setup can be
  folded in as you go. A slow warm-up loses people in the first ten seconds.
- **Each return means more.** The chorus comes back three times; each time the verses in between should change what it
  means. A chorus that means the same thing at the end as at the start has been repeated, not developed.
- **End changed.** The last section is the biggest, or the most transformed, or the quietest on purpose. When it echoes
  the first, something about it has changed.

## Specific, not general

A specific image ("the mug still on the desk") carries more feeling than a general statement ("I miss you"). Specifics
are where the film gets its pictures. The story itself should be easy for anyone to follow, with a few specific
references to the topic as seasoning, for people who know it, without losing those who don't (`craft/lyric.md`).
Details too precise to sing (numbers, names, small in-jokes) go into the picture, for those who pause.

## Show, don't tell

The film shows what the lyric implies and implies what the lyric says outright. If the line is "I left", showing someone
leaving is the weakest version; showing what's left behind, or the empty space where they stood, often lands harder.
Ask of each line: what's the image that makes someone feel it, not the image that illustrates it?

## Contrast

Feeling comes from difference: before and after, crowd and alone, colour and grey, fast and still. Plan contrasts at
every scale: within a shot, between neighbouring shots, between sections, between the start and the end. When a line
pairs two things ("the work you hate, the work you love"), show both halves.

## Point of view

Decide who's singing, to whom, and who the camera belongs to. The song's "I" and the film's lead don't have to be the
same person, but the relationship must be clear and consistent. A shift in point of view is a big event; use it for the
turn.

## Motifs

A thing that returns (an object, a colour, a gesture, a place) ties the film together and can carry the arc by changing
each time it appears. One or two strong motifs are better than many. *Check:* where does each motif appear, and what's
different about it each time?

## Modes of music video

Choose deliberately, or mix:

- **Narrative:** characters and events that tell the story. Strongest when the plot is simple enough to follow without
  words.
- **Concept:** one strong visual idea developed through the song (a world that floods, a room that keeps changing).
- **Performance:** the singer or band performing, intercut or alone. In a made film, the "performer" can be a character,
  an object, or the type itself.
- **Lyric-led:** the words are the main image, staged in the world or graphically.
- **Abstract:** shape, colour and motion following the music's structure and feeling.

## Things to avoid unless the prompt asks

A narrator who explains the story, a story about the making of the story, a mention that a model or tool made the song
or the film, an added mascot or recurring character the prompt didn't ask for, names given to unnamed people.
