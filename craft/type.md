# Words on screen

Whether the lyric appears on screen is a choice of the treatment. A lyric-led film puts the words at the centre; a
narrative film may show only a few key words, or none. When words appear, they're part of the picture, designed with
the shot, never laid on top of it as an afterthought. The shot comes first; the words are placed into it.

## Where words live

- **In the world:** painted on a wall, lit on a sign, typed on a screen, carved, stitched, written in the sky, spelled by
  objects or people. They obey the scene's perspective, light and physics. They feel like part of the story.
- **Graphic, on the frame:** clean type that moves as a design element, slams in on a beat, or takes over the frame for
  a transition. Bold and direct; fits graphic and typographic styles.
- **As the image:** a word large enough to be the set, which the camera moves through or around.

Vary the treatment from line to line by what each line means: the same placement for every line becomes wallpaper.

## Reading

- **Size and contrast.** Words meant to be read need to be big enough to read on a phone and separated from what's
  behind them by value. Saturated, solid type reads better than glowing, outlined or translucent type.
- **Time.** A line needs to be on screen long enough to read: roughly its sung duration. A word shown for a few frames
  isn't read.
- **Still enough to read.** Type moving fast across the screen, under heavy motion blur or out of focus can't be read.
  When the camera whips, the words being read are either gone or travel with the eye.
- **One place per word.** A word shouldn't appear in two places at once.
- **Spelled as sung** ("gonna", "'cause") when the song sings it that way.

## Timing

Each word appears on its sung onset (`song/timing.json`), not before the voice reaches it and not when the lyric sheet
says. Showing a line whole and filling or lighting each word as it's sung is easy to follow. A word sung across two
notes can build in two parts. Letter-by-letter reveals lag behind the voice; use them only when slowness is the point.

## How words arrive

Words that materialise in place (assemble, light up, are written, are revealed by the light or the camera) are easier to
follow than words that fly in from off-screen. Their arrival can echo the line's meaning: a word that shatters, melts,
stamps down, grows. Leaving matters too: words can be passed by the camera, fade with the light, or become the next
thing.

## When the words and the shot disagree

If keeping a word readable would ruin a camera move, change how the word appears: earlier, later, somewhere else, or
not at all. Never bend the camera to keep words in frame.

## Secondary text

Small text in the world (screens, signs, papers, labels) is a different layer: quiet, small, rewarding a pause. It never
competes with the line being sung.

## Fonts

Use fonts licensed for video (the SIL Open Font License covers most Google Fonts). In 3D, convert variable fonts to the
specific weight you use, so they render the same every time. Choose type to match the look: its era, its texture, its
weight.
